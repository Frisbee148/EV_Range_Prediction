## Physics-Informed LSTM for Multi-Vehicle EV Energy Consumption and Remaining-Range Prediction

# 1. Executive Summary

This project develops a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) built on an LSTM sequence backbone to predict remaining driving range for electric vehicles, alongside physically consistent estimates of battery state of charge (SOC) and battery power.

Real-world multi-vehicle EV telemetry at the required fidelity is not obtainable for this project. The dataset is therefore generated by SUMO traffic simulation combined with a documented vehicle-dynamics model, applied to four EV configurations chosen to span a wide physical parameter range (mass, battery capacity, drag coefficient, frontal area, rolling resistance, drivetrain efficiency, auxiliary load).

The project's contribution is a controlled comparison between a data-only LSTM and a physics-informed LSTM, under conditions where physics regularisation can plausibly help: limited training data, sparse SOC supervision, sensor noise, and extrapolation to unseen vehicle configurations.

**The project explicitly does not claim that physics-informed learning helps in all settings.** It measures where it helps and where it does not.

---

# 2. Problem Statement

A purely data-driven sequence model can learn correlations between driving behaviour and energy consumption without any guarantee that its outputs are mutually consistent. Specifically, a model with separate SOC and power heads can emit an SOC trajectory that does not correspond to the integral of its own predicted power, and can emit SOC values outside `[0, 1]` or SOC that increases while the battery is discharging.

These are not accuracy failures — a model can score well on MAE while doing all of the above. They are consistency failures, and they are exactly what a physics-informed training objective is designed to eliminate.

The training objective is therefore:

```text
(L6)  L_total = L_data + lambda * L_physics
```

where `L_data` measures error against labels and `L_physics` measures violation of physical relationships **between the model's own outputs** (see §3.1 for why this distinction is the crux of the project).

# 3. Research Questions

## 3.1 Design note: why v1's formulation was degenerate

This section is retained in the PRD because the failure mode is easy to reintroduce during implementation.

v1 defined the physics loss as:

```text
L_power = mean( (P_hat - P_physics)^2 )
```

where `P_physics` was computed from `v`, `a`, `theta` and the vehicle parameter vector. All of those quantities are **model inputs**. `P_physics` is therefore a deterministic function of the input. The dataset's power *label* is generated by that same function of that same input. The two are numerically identical.

Consequence: `L_power` inside `L_physics` was an exact duplicate of the power term inside `L_data`. "LSTM + Physics" reduced to "LSTM with the power and SOC loss terms multiplied by `(1 + lambda)`". The headline experiment would have compared a model against a reweighted copy of itself.

**The fix, applied throughout this document:** the physics residual is defined over the model's *own predictions only*, using no ground-truth labels. It is a self-consistency constraint, not a second supervised target. See §12.2.

A second consequence follows. On noiseless data generated by the physics, a sufficiently trained data-only model will satisfy the physics automatically, because the physics is the data-generating process. Physics regularisation can only earn its keep when there is an **information gap** — insufficient data, sparse labels, noise, or distribution shift. §11 constructs those gaps deliberately.

## 3.2 Primary research question

> Under limited, sparse, or noisy supervision, does a physics-consistency-regularised LSTM predict EV remaining range more accurately and more physically consistently than an identically configured data-only LSTM?

## 3.3 Secondary research questions

> **RQ2.** Does physics regularisation improve extrapolation to vehicle configurations not seen during training?
>
> **RQ3.** Does physics regularisation improve extrapolation to driving regimes not seen during training (e.g. train on urban and arterial, test on highway)?
>
> **RQ4.** How much training data does the data-only model need to match the physics-informed model's performance? (The data-ablation crossover point.)
>
> **RQ5.** Does temporal attention after the LSTM improve accuracy or interpretability enough to justify its complexity?

RQ1, RQ2 and RQ4 are core. RQ3 and RQ5 are optional if time is constrained.

# 4. Scope

## 4.1 In scope

**Machine learning:** LSTM sequence model, sliding-window construction, per-timestep multi-head output, optional post-LSTM attention, physics-consistency loss, seeded repeated runs, train/validation/test evaluation.

**Vehicle physics:** aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance, grade force, inertial force with rotating-mass correction, wheel power, drivetrain efficiency, regenerative braking with efficiency and power cap, auxiliary load, discrete SOC integration, energy-based remaining range.

**Simulation:** SUMO network with elevation, per-vehicle route and driver-parameter variation, urban / arterial / highway scenarios.

**Evaluation:** MAE, RMSE, MAPE, physics residual magnitude, SOC-bound violation rate, SOC-monotonicity violation rate, cross-vehicle and cross-regime generalisation, data-ablation curves, seed variance.

## 4.2 Out of scope

Electrochemical battery PDEs; cell-level simulation; thermal dynamics; degradation and ageing; internal-resistance estimation; reinforcement learning; autonomous driving; CARLA; CAN-bus integration; Jetson / Raspberry Pi / embedded deployment; real-time inference requirements; QP / QUBO / QPSO optimisation; route optimisation; VRP; quantum optimisation.

These are possible future extensions. None is required for the core deliverable.

# 6. Physics Model

All equations below are exact as implemented. `src/physics/` must match this section line for line.

## 6.1 Force balance

```text
(E1)  F_aero[t]  = 0.5 * rho * C_d * A * v[t]^2

(E2)  F_roll[t]  = m * g * C_rr * cos(theta[t])

(E3)  F_grade[t] = m * g * sin(theta[t])

(E4)  F_accel[t] = lambda_rot * m * a[t]

(E5)  F_trac[t]  = F_aero[t] + F_roll[t] + F_grade[t] + F_accel[t]
```

Notes:

- `(E2)` includes the `cos(theta)` term omitted in v1. For grades under 10% the correction is under 0.5%, but it is one character of code and removes an unstated approximation.
- `(E4)` includes the rotating-mass factor `lambda_rot`, accounting for the rotational inertia of wheels, driveshaft and rotor that must also be accelerated. v1 used `F_accel = m*a`, which understates acceleration energy by 3–5%. Default `lambda_rot = 1.04` `[ASSUMED]`.
- `F_aero` uses `v^2` not `v*|v|` because reverse motion is not simulated. If reverse is ever added, change to `v*abs(v)`.

## 6.2 Wheel and battery power

```text
(E6)  P_wheel[t] = F_trac[t] * v[t]

      Propulsion case:
(E7)  P_batt[t] = P_wheel[t] / eta_dt + P_aux[t]
                  when P_wheel[t] >= 0

      Regenerative braking case, above cut-in speed:
(E8)  P_batt[t] = max( P_wheel[t] * eta_rg , -P_regen_max ) + P_aux[t]
                  when P_wheel[t] < 0 and v[t] >= v_regen_min

      Regenerative braking case, below cut-in speed (friction braking only):
(E9)  P_batt[t] = P_aux[t]
                  when P_wheel[t] < 0 and v[t] < v_regen_min
```

Notes:

- v1 stated that regen "must be handled separately" without specifying how. `(E8)` and `(E9)` specify it.
- The `max(..., -P_regen_max)` clamp is not optional. Without it, hard braking events in SUMO produce regen power spikes of several hundred kW, which are physically impossible and will corrupt both SOC labels and the model's power distribution.
- Sign convention throughout: **positive `P_batt` = discharge**. `P_batt` may still be positive during light braking if `P_aux` exceeds recovered power. This is correct behaviour.
- `eta_dt` and `eta_rg` are constants here. Real drivetrain efficiency is load- and speed-dependent and falls sharply at low load. This is a documented simplification, listed in §23.

## 6.3 Auxiliary load with temperature coupling

v1 listed ambient temperature as a model input but no equation consumed it, making it an inert feature that the model would either ignore or exploit spuriously. Either remove `T_amb` from the feature set entirely, or couple it. This document couples it:

```text
(E10) P_aux[t] = P_aux_base + k_hvac * abs(T_amb[t] - T_comfort)
```

with `T_comfort = 21 degC` `[ASSUMED]` and `k_hvac = 60 W/degC` `[ASSUMED]`. If the temperature coupling is dropped, set `k_hvac = 0` and remove `T_amb` from the input feature list in §10.

## 6.4 SOC integration

```text
(E11) SOC[t+1] = clip( SOC[t] - P_batt[t] * dt / E_usable , 0.0 , 1.0 )
```

Unit check, mandatory before any training run: `P_batt` in W, `dt` in s, `E_usable` in J. `W * s / J` is dimensionless. If `E_usable` is left in kWh or `P_batt` in kW, the SOC trajectory will be wrong by a factor of 3.6e6 or 1e3 and will look plausible enough to miss.

```text
(E12) E_rem[t] = SOC[t] * E_usable
```

# 7. Remaining-Range Label Definition

v1 defined range as `R = E_remaining / E_consumption_per_km` where the consumption rate was computed "for the relevant driving window". This is underspecified in three ways: the window is not defined, the quantity is undefined at a standstill where distance travelled is zero, and the resulting label depends entirely on an arbitrary hyperparameter choice.

Two concrete definitions follow. **Definition B is the primary label.** Definition A is computed as well, for comparison and because it is what a production range estimator would realistically use.

## 7.1 Definition A — instantaneous consumption-rate estimate

```text
(E13) e_bar[t] = EMA_W( P_batt[t] / max(v[t], v_min) )        [J/m]

      R_A[t]   = E_rem[t] / max(e_bar[t], eps)                [m]
```

where `EMA_W` is an exponential moving average with smoothing window `W = 300 s` `[ASSUMED]`, initialised from the first 60 s of the trip.

Validity guard: `R_A[t]` is only defined once the trailing window contains at least `d_min = 500 m` of motion. Timesteps before that are masked out of the loss and the metrics, not filled with a placeholder value.

## 7.2 Definition B — oracle distance-to-empty (primary label)

Because the data is simulated, the true remaining range is computable exactly. Take the vehicle's full trip trajectory, tile it cyclically, and integrate forward from timestep `t` until the accumulated energy equals `E_rem[t]`. The distance covered is the true remaining range under the assumption that the current drive cycle repeats.

```text
      Let  de[t] = P_batt[t] * dt            [J consumed at step t]
           dx[t] = v[t] * dt                 [m travelled at step t]

      Let  CE[n] = prefix sum of de over the cyclically tiled trip
           CX[n] = prefix sum of dx over the cyclically tiled trip

(E14) R_B[t] = CX[ n* ] - CX[ t ]
               where n* = min{ n > t : CE[n] - CE[t] >= E_rem[t] }
```

Implementation: tile the trip enough times to guarantee full depletion (compute the cycle's net energy per lap and divide `E_usable` by it, then add one lap of margin), build the two prefix-sum arrays once per trip, and resolve `n*` by binary search. Cost is `O(n log n)` per trip and runs in seconds. **No re-simulation in SUMO is required** — this is pure post-processing on the physics output.

Guard: if a tiled cycle has net-negative energy consumption (possible for a short downhill-dominated trip), `R_B` is undefined. Drop such trips from the dataset and log the count.

Stated assumption, which must appear in the final report: `R_B` assumes the current drive cycle repeats until depletion. This is the same assumption underlying EPA and WLTP range figures. It is an assumption, not ground truth about future driving.

## 8.2 Parameter provenance tiers

v1 stated "we must not invent missing vehicle parameters". The intent is right, but applied literally it makes the project impossible: manufacturers publish mass and battery capacity, sometimes `C_d`, rarely frontal area, and essentially never rolling resistance, drivetrain efficiency, regen efficiency, or auxiliary load. Three tiers replace the binary rule:

- **`SOURCED`** — manufacturer specification, homologation document, or peer-reviewed measurement. Record URL and retrieval date.
- **`DERIVED`** — computed from sourced quantities by a recorded formula. Example: frontal area estimated as `0.8 * width * height` from published dimensions. Record the formula and its source.
- **`ASSUMED`** — taken from a literature range with a cited midpoint. Record the range and the citation.

Typical literature ranges for the parameters that will always be `ASSUMED`:

| Parameter | Plausible range | Midpoint used |
|---|---|---|
| `C_rr` (passenger EV, dry asphalt) | 0.008 – 0.012 | 0.010 |
| `eta_dt` | 0.85 – 0.93 | 0.90 |
| `eta_rg` | 0.60 – 0.70 | 0.65 |
| `P_aux_base` | 0.3 – 1.5 kW | 0.7 kW |
| `lambda_rot` | 1.03 – 1.05 | 1.04 |

Because these are assumptions, §16.9 runs a sensitivity analysis over them. This converts the project's weakest point into a reported result.

## 8.3 Four-vehicle selection strategy

The four vehicles must span a wide physical parameter range, not represent the EV market statistically. Target profile:

Vehicle 2   compact / medium                    mid m, mid E_usable, low C_d

## 9.2 Elevation: the failure mode to avoid

SUMO networks carry z-coordinates only if they were built with them. A default generated network, or an OSM import without elevation, is perfectly flat. Grade is then zero at every timestep, `F_grade` contributes nothing, and one of four physics terms is silently dead code for the entire project.

Verify before generating any data: run the simulation, load the FCD output, and assert `std(z) > 1.0` metres. If it fails, one of:

1. Import OSM with elevation: `netconvert --osm-files <file> --osm.elevation true` with SRTM/ASTER heightmap data supplied.
2. Synthesise a grade profile as a deterministic function of edge position, e.g. a sum of sinusoids with realistic amplitude (peak grade 4–8%), applied consistently per network. Document it as `[ASSUMED]` terrain.

Option 2 is acceptable and simpler. Option 1 is more defensible. Pick one and state it in the report.

## 9.3 Trajectory diversity: the second failure mode to avoid

SUMO's car-following behaviour is largely insensitive to vehicle mass. If all four vehicles are run on the same routes with the same seeds and the same vType dynamics, they produce **near-identical speed traces**, and the dataset becomes four copies of the same trajectories with only the label arithmetic differing. The model can then learn the closed-form parameter-to-label mapping directly, and the cross-vehicle experiment (§16.5) measures much less than it appears to.

Mitigations, all required:

- Different random seeds per vehicle per scenario.
- Different route sets per vehicle (sample from a larger route pool).
- Per-vehicle vType dynamics in the config: `max_speed_mps`, `accel_mps2`, `decel_mps2`, `sigma`. A heavy SUV genuinely accelerates more slowly than a light hatchback; set these to reflect that.
- Use the IDM car-following model rather than the default Krauss. Krauss produces unrealistically sharp acceleration transients that dominate `F_accel`.

## 9.4 Cross-check against SUMO's own battery device (recommended)

SUMO ships a battery device (`device.battery` on a vType) implementing the same drag / rolling / grade / inertia formulation, with parameters that map almost one-to-one onto §8.1: `vehicleMass`, `frontSurfaceArea`, `airDragCoefficient`, `rollDragCoefficient`, `propulsionEfficiency`, `recuperationEfficiency`, `internalMomentOfInertia`, `constantPowerIntake`. It emits per-timestep energy and SOC via `--battery-output`.

**Do not use it to replace the project's own physics engine** — labels and physics residual must come from the same documented implementation, and delegating to SUMO would reintroduce an opaque dependency.

**Do use it as an independent verification of the physics engine.** Run one trip through both, and assert agreement within 1–2% on cumulative energy. This catches unit errors, sign errors, and regen-handling errors immediately, and produces a clean validation paragraph for the report. Budget: about half a day. It is the highest-value half-day in the project.

## 9.5 Driving scenarios

Each vehicle is simulated under, at minimum:

| Scenario | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Urban | frequent stops, 0–50 km/h, high accel/decel density |
| Arterial | moderate stops, 30–70 km/h, mixed traffic |
| Highway | few stops, 80–120 km/h, aerodynamic drag dominant |

Optional stress scenarios: sustained steep grade, aggressive driver profile (high `accel`, low `sigma`), congested traffic, low ambient temperature (−5 degC) and high ambient temperature (38 degC).

Target volume: 4 vehicles × 3 scenarios × 20 trips × ~600 s ≈ 144,000 timesteps at `dt = 1 s`. This is ample. Do not over-generate; see §21 on compute.

# 11. Constructing the Information Gap

Per §3.1, physics regularisation cannot help on abundant, clean, physics-generated data, because the data already contains the physics. These three mechanisms create conditions where it can. **At least §11.2 must be implemented; §11.1 and §11.3 are strongly recommended.**

## 11.1 Data ablation

Train every model on `{5%, 10%, 25%, 50%, 100%}` of available trips, sampled at trip granularity, with the same trips across models and seeds. Plot Range MAE against training fraction for Model A and Model B.

Expected and reportable result shape: the physics-informed model outperforms at small fractions and the two converge as data grows. The crossover point is the answer to RQ4 and is a better headline figure than any single-row table.

## 11.2 Sparse SOC supervision

Supervise SOC only every `k` timesteps, with `k` in `{1, 10, 60, 300}`. Unsupervised timesteps have `soc_label_mask = 0` and are excluded from `L_data`, but the physics consistency residual `(L2)` still applies at every timestep.

This is both the most realistic setting and the one where a PINN is genuinely useful: a real BMS reports SOC coarsely and with quantisation, while speed and acceleration are available continuously. At `k = 300` the physics-informed model has a structural advantage that the data-only model cannot replicate.

## 11.3 Sensor noise

Optional but valuable. Apply, with a recorded seed:

| Quantity | Noise model |
|---|---|
| `speed_mps` | additive Gaussian, sigma = 0.3 m/s |
| `accel_mps2` | additive Gaussian, sigma = 0.15 m/s^2 |
| `grade_rad` | additive Gaussian, sigma = 0.005 rad |
| `soc` label | quantise to 1% steps, then additive Gaussian, sigma = 0.005 |

Noise is applied to the *features and labels only*, never to the quantities used inside the physics residual at training time — the residual operates on the model's own outputs and needs no clean reference.

# 12. Model and Loss

## 12.1 Architecture

```text
   dynamic features [B, T_seq, F_dyn]      static vehicle params [B, F_stat]
                  |                                      |
                  |                            broadcast over T_seq
                  |                                      |
                  +------------------ concat ------------+
                                      |
                          input projection (Linear + ReLU)
                                      |
                              LSTM (2 layers, H=128)
                          returns output at EVERY timestep
                                      |
                        optional temporal self-attention
                                      |
                       +--------------+--------------+
                       |              |              |
                  SOC head       Power head     Range head
                  per-timestep   per-timestep   per-timestep
                       |              |              |
                  SOC_hat[t]     P_hat[t]       R_hat[t]
                  for all t      for all t      for all t
```

**Critical design point:** the LSTM returns its output at every timestep, and heads are applied per-timestep. This is a change from a typical last-step-only design and is what makes the consistency residual `(L2)` computable — it needs `SOC_hat[t]` and `SOC_hat[t+1]` from the same forward pass. It also provides `T_seq` times more supervision signal per window.

Static parameters are concatenated to every timestep rather than injected once, so the model can condition its dynamics on vehicle identity throughout the sequence.

## 12.2 Loss definition

```text
      Per-head normalisation weights, computed once on the TRAINING split only:
(L0)  w_soc = 1 / var(soc_train)
      w_pow = 1 / var(p_batt_train)
      w_rng = 1 / var(range_b_train)

      Supervised data loss, with masking:
(L1)  L_data = w_soc * masked_mse(SOC_hat, soc,     soc_label_mask)
             + w_pow * masked_mse(P_hat,   p_batt,  1)
             + w_rng * masked_mse(R_hat,   range_b, range_valid_mask)

      Physics consistency residual -- uses NO ground-truth labels:
(L2)  r[t]       = SOC_hat[t+1] - ( SOC_hat[t] - P_hat[t] * dt / E_usable )
      L_consist  = mean over t in [0, T_seq-2] of r[t]^2

      Bound and monotonicity penalties:
(L3)  L_bounds   = mean( relu(-SOC_hat)^2 + relu(SOC_hat - 1)^2 )
                 + mean( relu(SOC_hat[t+1] - SOC_hat[t])^2  where P_hat[t] > 0 )
                 + mean( relu(-R_hat)^2 )

      Optional: consistency between the range head and the model's own SOC/power:
(L4)  L_range_c  = mean( ( R_hat[t] - SOC_hat[t] * E_usable / max(e_bar_hat[t], eps) )^2 )
                   where e_bar_hat is an EMA of P_hat[t] / max(v[t], v_min)

(L5)  L_physics  = L_consist + L_bounds + c_r * L_range_c

(L6)  L_total    = L_data + lambda(epoch) * L_physics

      with  lambda(epoch) = lambda_max * min( 1.0 , epoch / warmup_epochs )
```

Notes, each of which corrects or specifies something v1 left open:

- **`(L0)` normalisation is mandatory, not cosmetic.** SOC spans `[0,1]`, power spans roughly `[-1e5, 2e5]` W, range spans `[0, 5e5]` m. Unnormalised MSE is dominated by the range term by nine orders of magnitude, and any `lambda` sweep performed without `(L0)` is measuring nothing.
- **`(L2)` contains no ground-truth quantity.** `SOC_hat` and `P_hat` are both model outputs; `dt` and `E_usable` are known constants. This is the fix for §3.1 and is what makes the physics loss independent of the data loss.
- **`(L2)` is computable on unlabelled timesteps**, which is precisely what makes the sparse-supervision experiment (§11.2) meaningful.
- **`lambda` warm-up is required.** A randomly initialised network's SOC and power outputs are arbitrary, and applying the consistency penalty from step 0 produces gradients that fight the data loss and destabilise early training. Ramp linearly over `warmup_epochs`.
- **`(L4)` is optional.** Enable it only after `(L2)` and `(L3)` are working, and report it as an ablation. `c_r` defaults to 0.1.

## 12.3 The de-normalisation trap

The network consumes normalised features. `(L2)` requires `P_hat` in watts and `E_usable` in joules. **The physics residual must therefore be computed on de-normalised predictions**, either by inverting the scaler inside the loss function or by having the heads emit raw-unit values and normalising only the data-loss targets.

This fails silently if done wrong: the residual is simply large, `lambda` appears not to help, and the conclusion drawn is "physics does not work". Add a unit test that feeds a known physically consistent trajectory through `(L2)` and asserts the residual is below `1e-8`. Run it in CI.

# 13. Prediction Targets and Horizon

v1 never specified the time at which predictions are made relative to the input window. This is a first-order design decision and is fixed here.

**Core setup (nowcast + one-step):**

| Head | Target | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Power | `p_batt[t]` | same timestep as input `t`, for all `t` in the window |
| SOC | `soc[t]` | same timestep as input `t`, for all `t` in the window |
| Range | `range_b[t]` | same timestep as input `t`, for all `t` in the window |

The one-step relationship needed by `(L2)` comes from adjacent timesteps within the same window, not from a separate forecast head.

**Extended setup (optional, addresses the "why an LSTM at all" objection):** add a forecasting head predicting `soc[t + H]` for `H` in `{60, 300}` timesteps, using only inputs up to `t`. Nowcasting SOC and power is close to a memoryless function of the current input, which means a well-fitted MLP would do nearly as well as an LSTM and the sequence model is hard to justify. Forecasting is a genuine sequence problem. If time permits, include it; it materially strengthens the report.

# 14. Sequence Construction, Splitting and Normalisation

## 14.1 Windows

Sliding windows of length `T_seq` with stride `S`:

```text
window_i = rows[ i*S : i*S + T_seq ]   within a single trip, never spanning trips
```

`T_seq = 60` default (60 s of history at `dt = 1 s`). v1 suggested 30, which is short for anything range-related. Sweep `T_seq` in `{30, 60, 120}` (§16.8).

**`S = 5`, not 1.** Stride-1 windows give 99% overlap: a dataset five to ten times larger with almost no additional information, five to ten times the epoch time, and a wildly optimistic apparent sample count. Stride 5 is the single highest-leverage compute decision in the project.

## 14.2 Splitting

Split at **trip** granularity, never at window granularity. Windows from the same trip share timesteps, so random window-level splitting leaks test data into training and inflates every metric.

```text
all trips  ->  train 70% / validation 15% / test 15%
               stratified by (vehicle_id, scenario)
```

For the cross-vehicle experiment (§16.5), splitting is by vehicle instead, and the stratified split applies only within the training vehicles.

## 14.3 Normalisation

Fit `StandardScaler` on the **training split only**, then transform train, validation and test. This applies separately to:

1. dynamic features,
2. static vehicle parameters,
3. each target head.

Persist all scalers to `models/scalers/` alongside the checkpoint. A checkpoint without its scalers is unusable, and this is a common way to lose a completed experiment.

# 16. Experiments

Core experiments are E1, E2, E5, E7. The rest are optional in priority order E3, E8, E9, E6, E4.

## 16.1 E1 — In-distribution baseline

All four vehicles, all scenarios, full clean data, trip-level split. Establishes that every model trains and that Base-1 is beaten.

## 16.2 E2 — Data ablation (core, addresses RQ4)

Training fractions `{5%, 10%, 25%, 50%, 100%}` × models `{A, B}` × 5 seeds = 50 runs. Primary output: Range MAE vs. training fraction, with mean and standard-deviation bands.

## 16.3 E3 — Sparse SOC supervision (addresses RQ1 directly)

SOC label interval `k` in `{1, 10, 60, 300}` × models `{A, B}` × 5 seeds. This is the cleanest test of whether the physics residual substitutes for missing labels.

## 16.4 E4 — Noise robustness

Noise on / off × models `{A, B}` × 5 seeds.

## 16.5 E5 — Leave-one-vehicle-out (core, addresses RQ2)

Four folds. Train on three vehicles, test on the fourth, rotating the held-out vehicle. Models `{A, B}` × 4 folds × 5 seeds = 40 runs.

Report per-fold results, not just the average. If physics helps on the heavy SUV fold but not the light hatchback fold, that is a finding, and averaging it away loses it.

## 16.6 E6 — Cross-regime generalisation (addresses RQ3)

Train on urban + arterial, test on highway. The distribution shift is in the aerodynamic term, which scales with `v^2` and is where physics should most obviously help.

## 16.7 E7 — Lambda sweep (core)

`lambda_max` over a log-spaced grid `{1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1, 1e0, 1e1}`, selected on validation Range MAE, 3 seeds each.

Must be run **after** `(L0)` normalisation is in place, otherwise the sweep is meaningless. Report the sweep curve, not only the selected value — showing where physics starts to hurt is as informative as showing where it helps.

## 16.8 E8 — Sequence-length sweep

`T_seq` in `{30, 60, 120}`, model B, 3 seeds.

## 16.9 E9 — Parameter sensitivity

Regenerate labels with `C_rr` and `eta_dt` at the low, mid and high ends of their `[ASSUMED]` ranges from §8.2. Report how far range predictions and reported MAE move. This quantifies how much of the result rests on assumed parameters and is the honest answer to the most likely examiner question.

## 16.10 Seed protocol (applies to every experiment)

**Every reported number is the mean and standard deviation over at least 5 seeds.** v1 specified no repetition. A single-seed A-vs-B comparison on this data cannot be distinguished from run-to-run variance, and any conclusion drawn from one is unsupported. This is the single requirement that most determines whether the final claim survives scrutiny.

Report the paired difference `MAE_A - MAE_B` per seed, and state its mean and standard deviation, rather than comparing two independently averaged numbers.

# 17. Evaluation Metrics

## 17.1 Accuracy

```text
      MAE_x  = mean( abs(x_hat - x) )
      RMSE_x = sqrt( mean( (x_hat - x)^2 ) )
      MAPE_x = mean( abs(x_hat - x) / max(abs(x), eps) ) * 100
```

for `x` in `{range_b, soc, p_batt}`. Report range in km, SOC in percentage points, power in kW.

MAPE is reported for range only, where it is more interpretable than MAE across a 0–500 km span. It is not meaningful for power, which crosses zero.

## 17.2 Physics consistency

```text
      residual_rms      = sqrt( mean( r[t]^2 ) )                     from (L2)
      soc_bound_viol    = fraction of timesteps with SOC_hat outside [0,1]
      soc_mono_viol     = fraction of timesteps with SOC_hat[t+1] > SOC_hat[t] while P_hat[t] > 0
      range_neg_viol    = fraction of timesteps with R_hat < 0
```

These are computed for every model including Model A. Model A will typically score well on accuracy and poorly on consistency — that contrast is the clearest single result the project can produce, and it holds even if the accuracy difference is null.

## 17.3 Stratified reporting

Report range error stratified by SOC band `{100–75%, 75–50%, 50–25%, 25–0%}`. Error behaviour near depletion is what matters for the application and is masked by a single aggregate figure.

## 17.4 Results table format

```text
| Model  | Range MAE (km) | Range RMSE (km) | Range MAPE (%) | SOC MAE (pp) | Power MAE (kW) | Residual RMS | Bound viol (%) | Mono viol (%) |
|--------|----------------|-----------------|----------------|--------------|----------------|--------------|----------------|---------------|
| Base-0 |                |                 |                |              |                |              |                |               |
| Base-1 |                |                 |                |              |                |              |                |               |
| A      |                |                 |                |              |                |              |                |               |
| B      |                |                 |                |              |                |              |                |               |
| C      |                |                 |                |              |                |              |                |               |
```

Every cell is `mean ± std` over seeds. **No performance improvement is stated anywhere in the report before the corresponding experiment has been run.**

# 21. Compute Requirements

**A GPU is not required for this project, and no hardware purchase or paid cloud is needed.**

Sizing: roughly 144,000 timesteps; with `T_seq = 60` and `S = 5` that is approximately 28,000 training windows. A 2-layer LSTM with hidden size 128 is a small model. On a modern multi-core laptop CPU this is on the order of 30–60 seconds per epoch at batch size 256, so a 100-epoch run completes in about one to two hours.

Recommended plan:

1. **Develop entirely on CPU.** Use one vehicle, two trips, and 5 epochs to shake out shape errors, unit errors, the de-normalisation trap (§12.3), and the leakage tests. This phase is where most of the calendar time goes and none of it is compute-bound.
2. **Keep `S = 5`.** This alone is a 5× reduction in epoch time versus stride 1, with negligible information loss.
3. **Set `torch.set_num_threads()` to the physical core count** and use batch size 256–512. Small LSTMs on GPU are frequently kernel-launch-latency bound, so a large batch is what makes GPU worthwhile at all.
4. **Run the full experiment grid on free Colab or Kaggle.** The grid across E1, E2, E5 and E7 is roughly 150 runs. Kaggle provides around 30 GPU-hours per week on T4 or P100 hardware, which is sufficient. Checkpoint to Drive so a disconnect does not cost a run.

Device selection stays automatic:

```python
device = "cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu"
```

Note that SUMO simulation and preprocessing are single-threaded CPU work and will likely consume more wall-clock time than training. A GPU does not help there at all.

# 23. Known Limitations

To be stated explicitly in the final report, not buried:

1. **All data is simulated.** No claim is made about real-world accuracy. SUMO trajectories are a model of driving, not driving.
2. **Labels are generated by the same physics the model is regularised toward.** The project addresses this by constructing information gaps (§11), but the setting remains fundamentally more favourable to physics-informed methods than real telemetry would be. This is the project's most important caveat.
3. **Several vehicle parameters are assumed, not sourced** (§8.2). Sensitivity is quantified in E9 but not eliminated.
4. **Drivetrain and regen efficiencies are constants.** Real efficiency varies strongly with load and speed.
5. **No battery internal resistance, thermal behaviour, or ageing.** SOC integration is purely energy-based.
6. **Range label B assumes cycle repetition** (§7.2). This matches EPA/WLTP methodology but is not a prediction of actual future driving.
7. **Four vehicles do not represent the EV fleet.** They span a parameter range; they are not a statistical sample.
8. **Terrain may be synthetic** if §9.2 option 2 is taken.

E5 (leave-one-vehicle-out) or E2 cross-config ablation. Keep the SUMO XML scaffold
only (PRD §19.1); treat CSVs as reference.

## 28.3 RQ2 upgrade

Train on simulation (Tier C); test on held-out TUM BMW i3 telemetry (Tier A).
BMW i3 must be one of the four configured vehicles. This retires limitation #1 in
§23 as a hard blocker and gives the physics loss a real information gap
(noise + model mismatch) instead of only the synthetic gaps in §11.

## 28.4 First-week gate

1. Confirm reference net elevation (done: flat → use MoST).
2. Download TUM; check `∫ I V dt` vs SOC drop.
3. Run `(E1)`–`(E11)` on one TUM speed/elevation trace; require predicted `P_batt`
   within ~10–15% of measured before writing model code.
4. Fill `configs/vehicles.yaml` from EPA for all four vehicles.
